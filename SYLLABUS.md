# Biblical Apologetics: A 12-Week Self-Study

**Student:** Dr. Tom Hastings · **Format:** self-paced, about 1 unit per week · **Workload:** expect 6–10 hours in heavier units (Units 4, 8, 11, 12) · **Draft v2.2, 2026-11-03 (revised with Emerald)**

## Purpose
This course studies how Scripture defends itself: how God, Jesus, and the apostles argued for the truth, and what those arguments ask of you. The approach is 100% Biblical. Scripture is the final authority, and every other reading serves the text, the way Paul used Aratus in Acts 17:28: a borrowed line, put to work for the gospel.

## Method (every unit)
1. **Read** the primary passages in context. Read the whole chapter, not just the verses.
2. **Word study:** look up the listed Greek and Hebrew terms (STEP Bible, NET notes).
   > *Safeguard:* a word's possible range does not determine its meaning in a passage; context does. Do not argue from an English gloss, from etymology, or from every possible meaning of a lemma.
3. **Supplement:** one secondary reading or, in synthesis units, a focused review of earlier material.
4. **Questions:** answer them in a study journal, including the recurring *posture* question.
5. **Paper:** 1–2 pages defending a thesis drawn from the text.
6. **Reflection:** one page answering "What did this show me about God?"

## Unit 1: The Mandate and the Posture
- **Read:** 1 Pet 3:8–17; Jude 3–4; Phil 1:7, 12–18; Acts 22:1; 25:16; 2 Tim 2:24–25; Col 4:6
- **Why these readings:**
  - **1 Pet 3:8–17.** This is the anchor for our course. Peter tells you to be ready with a defense, but he sets it inside a passage about suffering and a good conscience.
  - **Jude 3–4.** Jude planned to write about salvation and switched to "contend for the faith." Pay particular attention to verse 4: people inside the church were bending the truth. Unfortunately, some of our defense is aimed at insiders, not only skeptics.
  - **Phil 1:7, 12–18.** Paul uses *apologia* for his own defense of the gospel while in chains. His trial ends up spreading the message.
  - **Acts 22:1; 25:16.** *Apologia* in a courtroom: a formal answer to charges. Understanding the word's everyday meaning is important before Peter applies it to every believer.
  - **2 Tim 2:24–25.** How to argue: kind, patient, not quarrelsome, correcting with gentleness. Use these as a lens next to 1 Pet 3:15.
  - **Col 4:6.** One verse on speech "seasoned with salt." It's short on purpose and it's foundational to every unit.
- **Words:** *apologia* (defense), *elpis* (hope), *prautēs* (gentleness), *phobos* (fear/reverence)
- **Supplement:** DTS *The Table*, "Helping Your Church Explore Faith Questions" (Mikel Del Rosario) — [Listen to the episode](https://voice.dts.edu/tablepodcast/apologetics-in-the-church/)
- **Questions:** What was an *apologia* in a first-century courtroom? Why does Peter put "set apart Christ as Lord" *before* "give a defense"? Who is being defended: the believer, the gospel, or God?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** "Apologetics is first an act of worship, then an act of argument."

## Unit 2: How We Know (Biblical Epistemology)
- **Read:** Prov 1:1–7; 9:10; Col 2:1–10; 1 Cor 1:18–2:16; John 17:17
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Prov 1:1–7; 9:10.** Proverbs states the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge. This claim has to be the foundation before you ask how anyone knows anything.
  - **Col 2:1–10.** All the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in Christ, and Paul warns against philosophy built on human tradition.
  - **1 Cor 1:18–2:16.** This is the most challenging reading this week. Paul seems to reject human wisdom, yet he argues carefully the whole way through. Working out how both are true is the main point of the unit.
  - **John 17:17.** "Your word is truth." Jesus says where final authority sits. The rest of the unit leans on this verse.
- **Words:** *yirʾat YHWH* (fear of the LORD), *daʿat* (knowledge), *sophia* (wisdom), *gnōsis* (knowledge), *alētheia* (truth)
- **Supplement:** Poythress, *Logic*, chs. 3 "What Do We Trust?" and 7–10 ("Logic Revealing God," "Logic as Personal," "Logic within Language," "Suppressing the Truth") — [Read the free PDF](https://frame-poythress.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/BLogicFinal.pdf). Optional: Calvin, *Institutes* 1.1–1.2 — [Read on CCEL](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.html)
- **Questions:** In what sense is the fear of the LORD the *beginning* of knowledge? Where are "all the treasures of wisdom" hidden (Col 2:3)? Is Paul anti-intellectual in 1 Cor 1–2?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** Is it circular to ground reason in God's revelation? Is every worldview's ultimate authority self-attesting?

## Unit 3: What Everyone Knows (Natural Revelation)
- **Read:** Ps 19; Rom 1:18–2:16; Acts 14:8–18; Eccl 3:11
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Ps 19.** Creation speaks in verses 1–6 and the law speaks in verses 7–14. One psalm shows you what nature reveals and what it can't.
  - **Rom 1:18–2:16.** The main text on what everyone knows about God and suppresses. Continue reading further into chapter 2, because the argument about conscience concludes at 2:16.
  - **Acts 14:8–18.** Paul at Lystra, talking to pagans who have no Bible. He argues from rain, harvests, and food. It shows natural revelation in a missionary encounter.
  - **Eccl 3:11.** God "has set eternity in the human heart." It explains why people keep asking questions they can't answer on their own.
- **Words:** *phaneros* (evident), *katechō* (suppress), *syneidēsis* (conscience), *anapologētos* (without excuse)
- **Supplement:** Calvin, *Institutes* 1.3–1.5 (*sensus divinitatis*) — [Read on CCEL](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/institutes.html)
- **Questions:** What exactly is "clearly seen" (Rom 1:20)? What does suppression look like in practice? How does Paul adapt this in Lystra?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** What knowledge of God does Romans 1 attribute to every person? What does the passage not establish?

## Unit 4: God's Case Against Idols (Idolatry and Worldview Critique)
- **Read:** Isa 40–48 (focus on 41:21–29; 44:6–20; 46:8–11); 1 Kgs 18:16–40; Ps 115; Jer 10:1–16
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Isa 40–48.** God puts the idols on trial and dares them to predict the future. It's the longest courtroom argument in Scripture.
  - **1 Kgs 18:16–40.** Elijah on Carmel. A public test with rules that both sides accept, and the false god fails.
  - **Ps 115.** Isaiah shows how idols get made and Ps 115 adds what they do to the people who trust them: "those who make them become like them."
  - **Jer 10:1–16.** A craftsman cuts down a tree, decorates it, and nails it in place so it won't tip over. Jeremiah shows that exposing a false belief can be part of a defense.
- **Words:** *ʾĕlîl* (worthless idol), *ʿēd* (witness), *rîb* (lawsuit)
- **Supplement:** John Frame, "Presuppositional Apologetics" (2012) — [Read the article](https://frame-poythress.org/presuppositional-apologetics/)
- **Questions:** What tests does God set for the idols in Isa 41? How does Isa 44 use reductio ad absurdum? What made Carmel a fair test?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** Isaiah's courtroom as a model for apologetics today: which features should Christians imitate, and which depend on God's unique authority to predict and act?

## Unit 5: Prophecy and Fulfillment
- **Read:** Deut 18:15–22; Isa 52:13–53:12; Ps 22; Mic 5:2; Dan 9:24–27; Luke 24:13–49; Acts 8:26–40
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Deut 18:15–22.** The test for a prophet: if it doesn't happen, the LORD didn't say it. Every fulfillment claim in this unit has to pass it.
  - **Isa 52:13–53:12.** The suffering servant. The NT applies it to Jesus by name (Acts 8:32–35), so you can see the fulfillment argument in the text instead of guessing at it.
  - **Ps 22.** Jesus quotes its first line from the cross. Read the whole psalm and notice how much of the crucifixion scene is already there.
  - **Mic 5:2.** Bethlehem as the ruler's birthplace. Matthew quotes it (Matt 2:5–6), which makes it one of the explicit fulfillment texts for your paper.
  - **Dan 9:24–27.** A prophecy with a timeline. It's harder and more disputed than the others. It's here so you practice separating what the text says from what interpreters build on it.
  - **Luke 24:13–49.** On the Emmaus road Jesus explains himself from "Moses and all the Prophets." This is Jesus making the prophecy argument himself.
  - **Acts 8:26–40.** Prophecy used in a real conversation. Philip starts with the official's question, begins from Isa 53, and tells him about Jesus (8:35).
- **Words:** *nābîʾ* (prophet), *plēroō* (fulfill), *dei* ("it is necessary")
- **Supplement:** NET notes on Isa 53 and Ps 22 — [Read NET notes: Isa 53](https://netbible.org/bible/Isaiah+53) · [Read NET notes: Ps 22](https://netbible.org/bible/Psalms+22)
- **Questions:** What is the Deut 18 test? What does Luke actually tell us about how Jesus "opened the Scriptures" (Luke 24:27, 44–47), and what does he leave unsaid? How do direct prediction, typology, and promise–fulfillment differ? Why did Philip start with Isa 53?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** Trace the Old Testament passages explicitly quoted or clearly invoked in Acts 8:32–35, Matthew 2:5–6, and the passion accounts' use of Psalm 22. Classify each use as direct prediction, typology, or broader promise–fulfillment, explaining any overlap. Then use Luke 24:44–47 to propose what Jesus may have covered, clearly labeling that portion as inference.

## Unit 6: Eyewitness Testimony and Textual Reliability
- **Read:** Luke 1:1–4; John 19:35; 20:30–31; 21:24; 1 John 1:1–4; 2 Pet 1:16–21; Deut 19:15
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Luke 1:1–4.** The author, traditionally Luke, says he followed everything carefully and drew on what eyewitnesses handed down. It's a historian's statement of method from inside the NT.
  - **John 19:35; 20:30–31; 21:24.** John keeps telling you who saw what and why he wrote it down. Three short passages, one claim: this is testimony, written so you will believe.
  - **1 John 1:1–4.** "What we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, and our hands have touched." Witness of the physical, checkable kind.
  - **2 Pet 1:16–21.** Peter says they didn't follow clever myths; they were eyewitnesses. Then he grounds prophecy in God rather than human will. Both halves matter here.
  - **Deut 19:15.** A matter stands on two or three witnesses. This is the legal standard the NT writers are working with.
- **Words:** *martys* / *martyria* (witness), *autoptēs* (eyewitness), *asphaleia* (certainty), *mythos* (story, fable)
- **Supplement:** DTS *The Table*, "Reliability of the New Testament" (Darrell Bock with Dan Wallace) — [Listen to the episode](https://voice.dts.edu/tablepodcast/reliability-new-testament/). Alternative: selected lessons from DTS's free course [*Can You Trust the Bible?*](https://courses.dts.edu/courses/can-you-trust-the-bible)
- **Questions:** What does Luke claim about his method? Why does John state his purpose so explicitly? How does Peter set eyewitness testimony against "cleverly devised myths"? What does manuscript evidence establish about the *wording* we have, and how is that a separate question from whether the *witnesses* are trustworthy?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** How do these passages characterize the New Testament writings—as orderly account, eyewitness testimony, proclamation, or some combination? What do those claims establish, and what still must be tested?

## Unit 7: The Resurrection
- **Read:** 1 Cor 15 (all); Matt 28; John 20–21; Acts 2:22–36; 17:30–31
- **Why these readings:**
  - **1 Cor 15.** The earliest written list of resurrection witnesses, followed by Paul's argument that everything depends on it. Read all of it. Verses 12–19 are your paper.
  - **Matt 28.** The empty tomb, the guards' cover story, and the commission. Matthew answers an objection that was already going around (28:11–15).
  - **John 20–21.** Thomas asks for evidence and gets it. Jesus cooks breakfast on the beach. These physical details rule out a vision or a ghost.
  - **Acts 2:22–36.** Peter's first sermon, seven weeks later, in the city where it happened. He appeals to what his audience already knows.
  - **Acts 17:30–31.** In Athens Paul names the resurrection as God's proof to everyone. It ties the event to the call to repent.
- **Words:** *egeirō* / *anastasis* (raise / resurrection), *ōphthē* ("He appeared"), *paradidōmi* (deliver, as tradition), *aparchē* (firstfruits)
- **Supplement:** DTS *The Table*, "Investigating the Resurrection of Jesus" (Darrell Bock) — [Listen to the episode](https://voice.dts.edu/tablepodcast/investigating-resurrection-jesus/)
- **Questions:** Outline Paul's argument in 15:1–11 and 15:12–19. Why does he list living witnesses? What is at stake "if Christ has not been raised"?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** Lay out 1 Corinthians 15:12–19 as a conditional or reductio argument. Identify each premise, each consequence Paul draws, and what the argument assumes versus what it attempts to establish.

## Unit 8: Evil, Suffering, the Cross, and Hope
- **Read:** Job 1–2, 38–42; Habakkuk (all); Ps 73; Rom 8:18–39; Gen 50:20
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Job 1–2, 38–42.** The setup and the answer. You see the heavenly scene Job never sees. Then God answers from the whirlwind without disclosing that scene or giving Job the explanation the reader has. The syllabus skips the speeches in between because the frame is the point.
  - **Habakkuk.** Babylon is still coming, but Habakkuk moves from complaint toward waiting and worship (3:17–19). That movement is what this unit is after.
  - **Ps 73.** Why do the wicked do so well? The turn comes at verse 17, in the sanctuary. It's the honest version of the question.
  - **Rom 8:18–39.** Paul weighs present suffering against future glory and says nothing can separate you from God's love. It comes after the resurrection unit on purpose: the answer depends on Easter.
  - **Gen 50:20.** "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good." One event, two intentions. The clearest case in Scripture.
- **Words:** *tām* (blameless), *ʾĕmûnāh* (faithfulness, Hab 2:4), *dikaioō* (justify, declare righteous)
- **Supplement:** DTS BE547, Unit 4, Video 7: "The Undeserved Suffering of Job" (Larry Waters) — [Watch lecture](https://media.dts.edu/player/?course=BE547&unit=4&video=7) · [Read transcript](https://media.dts.edu/player/print-transcript.aspx?course=BE547&language=en-US&unit=4&video=7). Optional: Unit 8, Video 1, "Intro to the Problem of Evil and Suffering" (Doug Blount) — [Watch lecture](https://media.dts.edu/player/?course=BE547&unit=8&video=1) · [Read transcript](https://media.dts.edu/player/print-transcript.aspx?course=BE547&language=en-US&unit=8&video=1); DTS Voice, ["Why Does God Allow Suffering?"](https://voice.dts.edu/article/why-does-god-allow-suffering-dallas-theological-seminary/)
- **Questions:** Why does God never tell Job about chapters 1–2? What changes in Habakkuk between 1:2 and 3:17–19? With Unit 7's case for the cross and the empty tomb in view, how do they reshape the question (Rom 8:18–39)?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** What answer does God give Job, what explanation does He withhold, and is His response enough? Defend your answer from Job 38–42.

## Unit 9: Jesus' Argumentative Methods
- **Read:** Matt 12:22–37; 21:23–27; 22:15–46; John 5:31–47; 8:12–59; 10:22–39
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Matt 12:22–37.** Accused of working by Satan's power, Jesus shows the charge undercuts itself: a divided kingdom can't stand. A clean reductio.
  - **Matt 21:23–27.** Asked about his authority, Jesus asks about John's baptism. His question exposes why they're asking.
  - **Matt 22:15–46.** Four exchanges under pressure, four different moves: Jesus sidesteps a trap, corrects a bad assumption, names the central command, and then asks the question nobody can answer.
  - **John 5:31–47.** Jesus calls his witnesses: John the Baptist, his works, the Father, and the Scriptures. Unit 6's testimony argument, from Jesus himself.
  - **John 8:12–59.** A long, sharp exchange that ends with "before Abraham was, I am." Jesus doesn't soften a claim to win the room.
  - **John 10:22–39.** Jesus argues from Ps 82 and from his works. A short example of reasoning from the other side's own Scripture.
- **Words:** *peirazō* (test/tempt), *elenchō* (convict/expose), *hypokritēs* (hypocrite)
- **Supplement:** Poythress, *Logic*, ch. 4 "Formal Logic" (pp. 45–50) and ch. 16 "Fallacies and God" (pp. 123–133) — [Read the free PDF](https://frame-poythress.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/BLogicFinal.pdf)
- **Questions:** How does Jesus answer a question with a question (Matt 21:24–25)? In Matt 22:15–22, how does Jesus recognize the trap, reject its framing, and introduce a higher allegiance? What are the "four witnesses" of John 5?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** Choose three argumentative moves Jesus uses. For each, explain the problem He faced, how His response works, when a Christian might use a similar move, and where Jesus' unique knowledge or authority limits imitation.

## Unit 10: Acts as an Apologetics Handbook
- **Read:** Acts 2:14–41; 7; 13:13–48; 17:1–4, 16–34; 19:8–10
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Acts 2:14–41.** Peter to a Jewish crowd. He starts from Joel and the Psalms because his listeners already accept them.
  - **Acts 7.** Stephen retells history his accusers already accept and shows a pattern in it: Israel kept rejecting God's messengers. Agreed facts become the argument.
  - **Acts 13:13–48.** Paul in a synagogue. Hold it next to Acts 17: same gospel, different starting point.
  - **Acts 17:1–4, 16–34.** Paul "reasoning from the Scriptures" in Thessalonica, then quoting Greek poets in Athens. The contrast between the two is the unit's main study.
  - **Acts 19:8–10.** Two years of daily discussion in a rented lecture hall. Apologetics as a long, steady practice.
- **Words:** *dialegomai* (reason), *peithō* (persuade), *paratithēmi* (set before/demonstrate), *spermologos* ("seed-picker"; babbler)
- **Supplement:** Constable's *Notes on Acts* (DTS, free), the sections on Acts 13 and 17 — [Read the notes](https://soniclight.com/tcon/notes/html/acts/acts.htm). Optional: Bahnsen's analysis of Acts 17 in *Always Ready*
- **Questions:** Compare Paul's starting points in the synagogue (Acts 13) and the Areopagus (Acts 17). What stays the same? Why does he quote Aratus? Where does the Areopagus speech end, and why?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** Contextualization without compromise. In Acts 17:22–31, what does Paul adopt from his audience, what does he challenge, and what parts of his message remain nonnegotiable? Distinguish textual observation from broader rules you infer.

## Unit 11: Paul's Defenses
- **Read:** Acts 22–26
- **Why these readings:**
  - **Acts 22–26.** Paul speaks in his own defense in five settings: the Jerusalem crowd, the council, Felix, Festus, and Agrippa. Read them in order. The central claims stay consistent while the details and approach change with each audience.
- **Words:** *apologeomai* (defend oneself), *mainomai* ("you are mad," 26:24), *sōphrosynē* (sober truth)
- **Supplement:** Review your Unit 1 notes — [Revisit Unit 1](unit-01.html)
- **Questions:** How does Paul's defense change across Jerusalem, Felix, Festus and Agrippa? What role does his personal testimony play? Why does he appeal to Agrippa's belief in the prophets?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?
- **Paper:** Compare Paul’s testimony in Acts 22 and 26. What remains constant, what changes with the audience, what other support accompanies his story, and what can personal testimony establish—or not establish—by itself?

## Unit 12: Synthesis and Posture
- **Read:** 2 Tim 2:14–26; Col 4:2–6; 2 Cor 10:1–6; Eph 6:10–20; 1 Cor 13; Matt 5:43–48
- **Why these readings:**
  - **2 Tim 2:14–26.** Skip the word-fights, handle the word accurately, and correct opponents gently in the hope God grants repentance. Paul states the goal of an argument plainly.
  - **Col 4:2–6.** Prayer first, then wise conduct with outsiders, then gracious speech. The order is the lesson.
  - **2 Cor 10:1–6.** Paul tears down arguments and takes thoughts captive, and he appeals "by the meekness and gentleness of Christ" in the same paragraph.
  - **Eph 6:10–20.** The armor of God. The fight is spiritual, and Paul ends by asking for boldness to speak.
  - **1 Cor 13.** Without love, the best argument is noise. You need this before you finish.
  - **Matt 5:43–48.** Love your enemies. The person across the table isn't your enemy, and if they were, this would still apply.
- **Words:** *aichmalōtizō* (take captive), *ochyrōma* (stronghold), *epieikeia* (gentleness, 2 Cor 10:1), *ēpios* (gentle, 2 Tim 2:24), *halas* (salt)
- **Supplement:** Reread 1 Pet 3:15–16 — [Read 1 Pet 3:15–16 (NET)](https://www.bible.com/bible/107/1PE.3.15-16.NET)
- **Questions:** What weapons does Paul *refuse* to use? What is the goal in 2 Tim 2:25–26? Can you win an argument and lose a person?
- **Posture:** How should these texts shape the apologist's tone and conduct?

Develop a thesis covering the mandate, method, content, and posture of biblical apologetics. Draw from all twelve units, cite the primary passages, distinguish explicit textual claims from inference, and synthesize the material rather than summarizing one unit at a time.
